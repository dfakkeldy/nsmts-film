1. Cold open
This is Mabou in the 1880s, as Hugh Fletcher surveyed it. And this is Mabou today. I'm Dan Fakkeldy, and I built NS
Marks The Spot to put them on the same map.

2. Where the data lives
Nova Scotia publishes a lot of map data, but it's scattered: provincial ArcGIS services, open data portals, federal
and municipal servers. I know where it lives, how to query it, and what each licence allows. The map draws on more
than a hundred layers, and every one keeps its source and licence on screen.

3. Parcels
Pick a parcel and it checks roads, water, buildings, civic addresses and assessments, each against its own source,
and it tells you when a source comes back empty.

4. Georeferencing
Georeferencing is the heart of it. Drop in a scan, match a few landmarks, and it drapes onto today's map. Every point
shows how well it fits, and which one disagrees with the rest. Hand-drawn maps bend, so a curved warp passes through
every point. That's how twenty-four of Fletcher's sheets got onto the map.

5. Analysis
It also screens rivers for small hydro, and shows where a proposed wind farm could be seen from, on bare earth.

6. GeoPDF
Frame a map and export a georeferenced PDF [CHECK: "that QGIS and Avenza can open" only after those tests pass]. Bring
GeoPDFs in too, and see where you are on them.

7. Field
On a phone, it logs points, photos and tracks [CHECK on a real phone: "and keeps recording when the signal drops"].
It all stays on the device until you export it.

8. Poker
This one I use at work. Where's that house? Is the driveway too far? There's no signal at work, so I made it run
offline. My coworkers use it too. One day I couldn't find an address: not in the list, and no house on the aerial. I
know almost every address now. I built it for whoever starts next.

9. iOS
It's also a native iPhone app, in TestFlight.
